Hive Risk Orientation
Case Study

Over the course of ten weeks, our team of 5 designers conducted ethnographic research, built user personas and journey maps, and prototyped three distinct solutions before converging on a final concept that addresses the needs of both climbers and The Hive.

My Role

Researcher, UI/UX Designer

Timeline

10 Weeks

Tools

Figma, Photoshop, After Effects

Year

2024

⚠️ This is a student project and a concept prototype. This is not an official Hive Climbing product, nor is it endorsed by The Hive.

Overview

Rethinking how climbers learn to stay safe.

Before new climbers begin bouldering at any of The Hive's many climbing gyms, they must go through a safety orientation with a trained instructor.

While this method of teaching new climbers has high retention rates, it is very tedious and inconsistent. A digitized mandatory risk orientation could improve both user experience and liability protection by ensuring consistent safety training while maintaining a clear digital record of participant completion and acknowledgment.

Process

From observation to solution.

01

Brief

Hive Climbing came to us with a clear tension: their risk orientation process was time-consuming for staff, inconsistent across locations, and not always memorable for new climbers. Our brief was to explore how the orientation experience could be improved — whether through digital tools, physical redesigns, or process changes — without compromising on safety rigour.

02

Ethnographic Research

To begin this project, I conducted some ethnographic research to better understand the pain points of climbers and managers alike.

I drafted up questions for climbers and collected data through a survey. I also went onsite to multiple Hive locations across Metro Vancouver to gain a better understanding of how the staff feel about the current risk orientation process.

03

Constructing Personas

Through the results of our research, we realized that the experience level of different climbers might influence their appeal in a digitized risk orientation. User personas were constructed to better visualize the differences in climbers.

04

User Journeys

We came up with a couple user flows to help us picture what the common pain points and needs that different climbers might have during their risk orientation experience. These gaps became the design targets for our solution concepts.

05

Three Possible Solutions

We developed three distinct concepts to address the problem from different angles. Concept A was a self-guided kiosk orientation that members complete before speaking to staff, freeing up desk time. Concept B was a mobile-first pre-arrival orientation flow sent via QR code after booking, so members arrive already briefed. Concept C was a physical redesign of the orientation zone itself — using signage, floor graphics, and modular station cards to make the space do the teaching. Each concept was prototyped and tested with representative users.

06

Final Solution

The final solution combined the strongest elements of all three concepts. A pre-arrival mobile orientation flow handles foundational knowledge before members walk in, reducing in-person time. On arrival, redesigned signage reinforces key safety points passively. Staff retain a brief, focused confirmation conversation rather than delivering the full brief from scratch. The result is an orientation that's faster, more consistent, and more likely to actually be remembered.

Final Product

The concept, in full.

The final prototype spans three touchpoints — a pre-arrival mobile flow, redesigned in-gym signage, and a streamlined staff confirmation checklist — unified by a clear visual language and plain-language safety content.

Reflection

What we learned

Testing the final prototype with users showed measurable improvements in orientation completion time and self-reported confidence around safety rules. The project surfaced how much orientation design relies on timing — delivering information at the right moment, not just the right content.
