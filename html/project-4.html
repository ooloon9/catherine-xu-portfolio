Hive Risk Orientation
Case Study

Over the course of ten weeks, our team of 5 designers conducted ethnographic research, built user personas and journey maps, and prototyped three distinct solutions before converging on a final concept that addresses the needs of both climbers and The Hive.

My Role

Researcher, UI/UX Designer

Timeline

10 Weeks

Tools

Figma, Photoshop, After Effects

Year

2024

⚠️ This is a student project and a concept prototype. This is not an official Hive Climbing product, nor is it endorsed by The Hive.

Overview

Rethinking how climbers learn to stay safe.

Before new climbers begin bouldering at any of The Hive's many climbing gyms, they must go through a safety orientation with a trained instructor.

While this method of teaching new climbers has high retention rates, it is very tedious and inconsistent. A digitized mandatory risk orientation could improve both user experience and liability protection by ensuring consistent safety training while maintaining a clear digital record of participant completion and acknowledgment.

Process

From observation to solution.

01

Brief

IAT 333: Interaction Design Methods is a course centered around designing meaningful interactive experiences in collaboration with real-world businesses. Our group managed to get a hold of The Hive Climbing Gym, who had plans of implementing a digitized version of their pre-climbing mandatory risk orientation.

02

Ethnographic Research

To begin this project, I conducted some ethnographic research to better understand the pain points of climbers and managers alike.

I drafted up questions for climbers and collected data through a survey. I also went onsite to multiple Hive locations across Metro Vancouver to gain a better understanding of how the staff feel about the current risk orientation process.

The challenge for our group now was to balance user interest and engagement with knowledge retention.

03

Constructing Personas

Through the results of our research, we realized that the experience level of different climbers might influence their appeal in a digitized risk orientation. User personas were constructed to better visualize the differences in climbers.

04

User Journeys

We came up with a couple user flows to help us picture what the common pain points and needs that different climbers might have during their risk orientation experience. These gaps became the design targets for our solution concepts.

05

Three Solution Concepts

We developed three distinct concepts to address the problem from different angles, and I turned them into easy to digest graphics:

Concept A: Game Based Kiosk was a self-guided kiosk orientation that members complete before speaking to staff, freeing up desk time.

Concept B: Web Training Modules was a mobile-first orientation flow that climbers do after booking, but before climbing.

Concept C: QR Code Scavenger Hunt was a physical redesign of the orientation zone itself — using signage, floor graphics, and modular station cards to make the space do the teaching.

06

Final Solution

After feedback from our instructor and peers, we decided that the most effective solution that maintains user engagement and retention, as well as consistency is Concept A: Game-Based Quiz Kiosk. The final step for us as a team is to prototype the concept.

Final Product

Gameified Orientation

Inspired by applications like Khan Academy and Duolingo, our final prototype guides users through a detailed , step by step orientation with easy to understand visuals while keeping them engaged with an interactive segment that tests to see if they understood the material.

Reflection

What we learned

While gameifying the orientation does make the process feel more interesting, it is still a time consuming process for climbers. While this streamlines the process for the managers and instructors at The Hive, it is not yet optimized for the climbers who may end up using the product.
